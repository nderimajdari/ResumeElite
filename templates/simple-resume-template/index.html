The Simple resume template

A resume with nothing in it that is not information. Your name is centred, sections are separated by a hairline rule, and everything runs in one column from top to bottom. No photo, no icons, no sidebar.

  • Best forAny job, any industry, online application portals
  • StructureOne column, centred header, ruled sections
  • LengthOne page for most people; two for long careers
  • ATSSafest choice: single text flow, standard headings

Why simple works

A recruiter spends seconds on the first pass. On a simple layout their eye goes straight down the page: name, title, summary, the most recent job. Nothing competes for attention, so your content does all the work.

It is also the layout that survives every system it passes through. Applicant tracking systems, the "upload your resume" box on LinkedIn, a PDF opened on a phone, a page printed in black and white: one column of text in reading order comes through all of them unchanged.

What to put in it

In this order, which is the order a recruiter reads in:

  1. Header: name, the job title you want, email, phone, city, LinkedIn.
  2. Professional summary: two or three lines. What you do, for how long, and one result you are proud of.
  3. Work experience: most recent first. Title, employer, city, dates, then two to five bullets that start with a verb and end with a result.
  4. Education: degree, school, year. Move it above experience only if you graduated in the last two years.
  5. Skills: the ones the job posting asks for, in the posting's own words.

Languages, certifications and projects go at the end if they help this application.

When to choose something else

Simple is never the wrong answer, but it can be a missed chance. If you are a designer, a portfolio-led layout says more about you; if you are changing careers, a skills-first layout hides less well-matched job titles. You can switch at any time and your content moves with you.
