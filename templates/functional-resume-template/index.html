The Functional resume template

A skills-first resume. Core Competencies sit at the top of the page in a two-column checklist, followed by your strengths and achievements; Employment History comes after, kept short. Built for people whose job titles do not tell the story on their own.

  • StructureCompetencies and achievements first, jobs below

Who a functional resume is for

A reverse-chronological resume makes your latest job title the headline. That is a problem when the title is the wrong one: a teacher applying for instructional design, a parent returning after five years, a soldier moving into logistics management. A functional resume puts what you can do first and lets the job history support it.

How to fill it in

Core Competencies. Six to nine skills, taken from the job posting you are applying to. "Stakeholder communication" beats "good communicator".

Achievements. This is the section that makes a functional resume credible. Group two or three results under each competency, wherever you earned them:

Training design: built a 6-week onboarding course for 40 new staff; time to full productivity fell from 10 weeks to 7.

Budgeting: managed a $120,000 department budget and closed two years under plan.

Employment History. Every job, with employer, title and dates. Keep each one to a line or two. Leaving jobs out is what gave functional resumes a bad name; recruiters read missing dates as something to hide.

The honest trade-off

Many recruiters prefer chronological resumes because they can see what you did when. A functional resume is the right tool when chronology works against you, not a default. A useful middle ground: keep this layout but write two good bullets under each recent job, so the history carries weight too.
